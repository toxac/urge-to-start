Every successful project gets built partly because the founder learned how to make direct, clear requests for help, introductions, feedback, or early capital[cite: 2]. The individuals who fail stay stuck behind screen layouts forever because they cannot bring themselves to approach real humans[cite: 2].

Most bad outreach attempts fail before anyone finishes reading them because the language is wrapped in a soft, apologetic, defensive fog[cite: 2]. We approach busy people with vague statements like: *"I'd love to pick your brain sometime."* This places the entire burden of structuring the conversation directly on them, signaling that you haven't prepared your thoughts[cite: 2]. 

Before you approach anyone, you must get specific and clarify three parameters[cite: 2]:
1.  **What exactly do you need them to do?** (Isolate a singular question or request)[cite: 2].
2.  **How long will it take?** (If you ask for fifteen minutes, take exactly fifteen minutes)[cite: 2].
3.  **Why them?** (Explain why their specific experience matters to your current bottleneck)[cite: 2].

When you learn to ask well, you aren't just taking value—you are giving the other person a clean chance to show up and help someone build[cite: 2]. Most people actually want to do that; they just need to be asked clearly enough that they know how[cite: 2].
